# Patterns

Name the pattern, quote the line, give the fix. Word-level rules come from the user's own instructions, which are active already, so this file leaves them out.

**Binary contrasts.** 'This is not X. It's Y.', 'The question isn't X, it's Y.', 'It's not just X but Y.' Say Y and drop X. 'The problem isn't the price. It's the onboarding.' becomes 'Onboarding loses more customers than price does.'

**Throat-clearing openers.** 'Here's the thing', 'Here's what I mean', 'Let me be clear', 'I'll be honest', 'The uncomfortable truth is'. Delete the opener and start with the point.

**Faux-insight setups.** 'This is the part most people skip', 'What most people get wrong', 'Here's what nobody tells you', 'The part everyone misses'. The setup casts the writer as the only one who knows. Remove it and let the claim carry itself. 'What nobody tells you about hiring: the first ten people set the culture' becomes 'The first ten hires set the culture.'

**Colon reveals.** A noun phrase and a colon, followed by a lowercase payoff staged as drama, as in 'The secret ingredient: a weekly call' or 'The kicker: it's free.' Write the plain sentence instead: 'A weekly call keeps the project on track.' A colon introduces a list, a label or a quote. After a colon, use sentence case unless a title, a proper noun, code or grammar demands a capital.

**Superficial analysis.** A trailing '-ing' clause that claims to explain what something means: 'highlighting', 'underscoring', 'reflecting', 'showcasing'. Cut it, and give the concrete consequence if there is one. 'The update adds offline mode, underscoring the team's focus on reliability' becomes 'The update adds offline mode, so drafts survive a dropped connection.'

**Importance puffery.** 'Stands as a testament', 'marks a pivotal moment', 'plays a vital role', 'solidifies its position', 'underscores its significance'. Give the fact and let the reader weigh it. 'The hire marks a pivotal moment for the team' becomes 'The hire is the team's first designer.'

**Weasel attribution.** 'Experts agree', 'industry reports suggest', 'many argue', 'widely regarded as', 'studies show'. Name the source or drop the claim. When the user has no source, ask; never make one up.

**Fake-strong verbs.** An inflated verb such as 'serves as' where 'is' or 'has' reads clearer. Use the plain verb. 'The dashboard serves as a central hub for invoices' becomes 'The dashboard lists every invoice, its due date and who approved it.'

**Synonym cycling.** Swapping words for the same thing to avoid repetition. When one word is right, use it every time. 'The report covers Q3. The document flags two risks. The analysis ends with a plan' becomes 'The report covers Q3, flags two risks and ends with a plan.'

**Negative listing.** 'Not a X. Not a Y. A Z.' Write Z.

**Dramatic fragmentation.** 'X. And Y. And Z.' or 'That's it. That's the whole thing.' Rewrite as full sentences.

**Robotic rhythm.** The same sentence shape again and again, paragraphs built on one template, punchy fragments stacked in a row. Change the shape only where that serves the point.

**Rhetorical setups.** 'What if I told you...', 'Think about it:', 'Plot twist:', and a question the writer answers in the next breath. Remove the setup and make the point.

**Fake-profound kickers.** A closing line that turns the point into a metaphor, an aphorism or a mic drop. Delete it. Don't trade it for a better metaphor and don't keep its rhythm. End on the clearest concrete sentence the draft already has. If the ending still needs closure, add a plain takeaway or the next action.

**Summary-recap endings.** 'In conclusion', 'Ultimately', 'Overall', or a last paragraph that repeats the piece. The reader read it a moment ago. Stop on the final concrete point, a takeaway or the next action.

**Formatting slop.** Emoji in headings, bold scattered through sentences for emphasis, bullets where two sentences of prose read better, a header over a section of two sentences. Let the content decide the format.

**Em dashes.** In the user's own copy, their own rule on dashes wins. In a draft by someone else, remove clusters and decorative dashes. Keep one only where it does a better job than a comma, a period or parentheses.
