# Editing principles

How to cut slop without losing the writer.

- Know the job first. Before you touch structure or wording, know what the piece has to do and who reads it.
- Listen before you edit. Note how the draft sounds: the words it picks, its cadence, how blunt, funny or unsure it is, where it digresses and how polished it is. Keep whatever is personal to this writer. Don't tidy every paragraph to the same finish or rework a distinctive line for consistency.
- Change as little as the fix needs. Repair AI patterns, mistakes, repetition and unclear passages, and leave strong human sentences as they are. After your edit, a rough draft with a real voice still sounds like its author.
- Keep their meaning. Add no claim, example, statistic or opinion of your own. When something is unclear, ask.
- Make it easier to read without making it simpler. Keep the substance, nuance and precision. Remove what blocks the reader: jargon, overlong sentences, abstract nouns, tangled structure.
- Keep the structure unless it hurts the piece. A writer's progression and detours often carry their personality. If you reorganize, give the reason in the 'What changed' section.
- Start with the point when the lead-in adds nothing. Generic throat-clearing goes. A personal aside, story or admission stays when it brings context, tension or character.
- Put the conclusion first only where that helps the reader. Sections and paragraphs don't all need the order point, then detail, then background.
- Give every sentence a reason to stay. Cut empty qualifiers and throat-clearing. 'I think', 'maybe' and 'to be honest' stay when they carry genuine doubt, self-awareness or the rhythm of how this writer talks.
- Untangle without flattening. Split a sentence or paragraph that is hard to follow. Keep long spoken sentences, fragments and shifts in pace that are clear and sound like the writer.
- Write in the active voice. 'We shipped it Tuesday' beats 'the release happened'. Things don't take human actions.
- Let verbs do the work. Swap a weak verb phrase for the direct verb: 'decided' for 'made a decision', 'can' for 'has the ability to'.
- Be concrete. An abstraction loses to a name, a number, a date, a mechanism or an example. 'Deploys got much faster' becomes 'Deploys dropped from 40 minutes to 4.'
- Protect the specific fact. Never blur a useful detail into general importance. 'The tool boosts engineering productivity' becomes 'Code review went from 30 minutes to 8.'
- Keep the edge. Strong opinions, humor, blunt language, swearing, honest admissions and self-interruptions belong to the writer. Don't swap them for safer, more professional wording.
