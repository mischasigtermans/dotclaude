# Craft

Techniques by move, for a draft that reads generic. Each one says what it is, when to use it, and shows it. Quoted lines are verbatim from the post named after them; unattributed examples are generic illustrations. Borrow the move, never someone else's phrasing.

## Titles

**Title as thesis.** The title states the claim, and the first line restates it harder. Use it for opinion and framework pieces. 'A directory is worthless, a registrar isn't' (`a-directory-is-worthless-a-registrar-isnt`)

**A coined handle.** Two to four plain words, often a household word pinned to an abstract idea, so the reader can carry it away and argue with it. Use it when the piece proposes a way of thinking. 'The painkiller test' (`the-painkiller-test`)

**The number in the title.** A specific figure the reader wants explained. Use it when the number is the result, never as decoration. 'How I saved 4.7 billion tokens with a Claude Code hook' (`how-i-saved-billions-of-tokens-with-a-claude-code-hook`)

**Literal for how-tos.** Say what the reader will be able to do. Save the cleverness for the essay. 'How I run Claude Code with Solo and Library' (`how-i-run-claude-code-with-solo-and-library`)

Never: a superlative ('the ultimate guide'), a teaser ('you won't believe'), a keyword string.

## Openers

**The claim.** One declarative sentence; the rest of the piece defends it. Use it for framework and opinion pieces. 'Every AI demo you've seen in the last year looks the same.' (`generic-ai-is-a-commodity`)

**The direct report.** What happened, in one plain sentence. The thinking starts in sentence two. Use it when news or a reaction triggered the piece. 'Not everyone, but a lot of people in my X feed are saying the same thing this week.' (`opus-didnt-get-worse`)

**The object first.** Start with one concrete thing, a scene, a timestamp or a setting, and only later say what it means. Use it when the story is the argument. 'A DJ plays a Friday night slot at a festival.' (`nightshift-why-fridays-gig-is-on-saturday`)

**The number that needs context.** A figure so specific the reader wants the story behind it. Use it only when the number matters to the argument. '4.7 billion tokens saved.' (`how-i-saved-billions-of-tokens-with-a-claude-code-hook`)

**The concession, then the turn.** Grant the opposing view in full, then pivot. Use it when the reader likely arrives disagreeing; the concession buys the right to disagree back. 'This is legitimate criticism. The problem is real.' (`ai-skeptics-havent-shipped-anything`)

**Write it last.** Draft the claim, the closer and the headers first, fill the sections, then rewrite the opening once you know what the piece is about. Openings written first are why most drafts bury the thesis.

## Building the argument

**One idea per piece.** If the draft argues two things, split it or pick one. The reader leaves with one thing in their head.

**Report the practice, imply the advice.** Describe what you do as a house rule instead of telling the reader what to do. The reader overhears a working method, which persuades better than a lecture. 'Every week I make a list of what I'm tempted to build next, and I run the test on each item.' (`the-painkiller-test`)

**What I actually tried.** A paragraph where the theory stops and your own use starts. Any piece about a tool or a model needs one; without it the piece is a take. 'I use Opus every day for my actual job.' (`opus-didnt-get-worse`)

**Symptom, diagnosis, reframe.** Agree the symptom is real, name a different cause, restate the problem in its true terms. Use it against a popular but misread complaint. 'These are incentive problems wearing AI clothes.' (`ai-skeptics-havent-shipped-anything`)

**Skepticism with a wish attached.** Say what you want to be true, then what you see. It reads as grounded instead of cynical. Example: 'I want agents to run unattended. When I run three at once, one of them still stops to ask.'

**One load-bearing analogy.** A single comparison that replaces three paragraphs of explanation. One per piece. 'The model is the engine. The product is the chassis. Nobody buys a car for the engine alone.' (`generic-ai-is-a-commodity`)

**Name where your own idea breaks.** Give the edge case its own section. It's the credibility move that makes the rest believable. 'Every useful framework has an edge case where it's wrong' (`the-painkiller-test`)

**Credit by name.** When the idea isn't yours, say so, name the source and link it. Generosity reads as confidence. 'I didn't invent this framing.' (`the-painkiller-test`)

**Refuse 'it depends'.** Pick a side and state the conditions under which you'd be wrong, instead of listing considerations.

**Understate the verdict.** When something works, say so plainly and bound it to your use. Praise that's measured gets believed. 'None of this sounds revolutionary. That's the point.' (`ai-skeptics-havent-shipped-anything`)

**The half-life caveat.** When praising a fast-moving tool, note that the praise has an expiry date. Example: 'It's the best I've used. Ask me again after the next release.'

**Section rhythm.** Most argued pieces move through setup, an application to a real case, the pushback or edge case, the turn back, and the close. When a draft feels off, map it against these beats and find the missing one.

## Rhythm and sentence length

**Long, long, short.** One or two sentences develop the thought, a short one lands it. The default cadence. 'The ratio of painkillers to vitamins in your roadmap is the roadmap. Everything else is decoration.' (`the-painkiller-test`)

**The fragment as verdict.** A fragment judges what the long sentence argued. One at a time; a run of them reads like a slogan. 'Same pattern. Different decade.' (`ai-skeptics-havent-shipped-anything`)

**Triplets with three facts.** Three parallel beats work when each carries something new. 'Claude Code is the hands. Solo is the workbench. Library is the memory.' (`how-i-run-claude-code-with-solo-and-library`)

**Mirrored pairs.** Two sentences with the same shape and opposite content make a contrast stick. 'Painkillers have hunters. Vitamins have nodders.' (`the-painkiller-test`)

**'We' for actions, 'I' for convictions.** The team shipped it; you believe it.

## Cutting

**Cut a fifth.** When the draft feels done, cut 20% and see what dies. Usually nothing.

**Kill the setup paragraph.** If a paragraph only prepares the reader, rewrite the opening so it isn't needed.

**Ask what can go.** For every section: does the argument survive without it? If yes, it goes, however good it is.

**Drop the scaffolding on short pieces.** Under 800 words, H2s fragment more than they guide. Run it as one flow.

**Don't compress the essay into an aphorism.** Cutting tightens paragraphs. It doesn't strip the lived experience that makes the claim credible.

## Specifics and receipts

**The receipts paragraph.** A run of exact numbers, names and dates that carries the argument by itself. 'As I write this, it holds 2.778 conversations.' (`how-i-run-claude-code-with-solo-and-library`)

**The density test.** Every claim carries a number, a named product or a mechanism the reader could act on tomorrow. If you can't attach one without making it up, you don't have a take yet. Earned: 'Data is the hardest layer to copy because time is the only input and there's no shortcut.' (`generic-ai-is-a-commodity`) Fortune cookie: 'Never underestimate the power of data.'

**Exact over round.** '2.778', not 'nearly 3.000'. Share a number when it makes the point; don't turn the piece into a dashboard.

**The proportional triplet.** Break a whole into honest fractions. 'Half of them get dropped immediately. A quarter get deferred until the painkillers are stable. The rest are the roadmap.' (`the-painkiller-test`)

## Section headers

**H2 as mini-thesis.** Each header carries its section's claim. Read the headers alone; they should form the argument. 'The test that kills good ideas for the right reason' (`the-painkiller-test`)

**Name the edge case in the header.** Put the weakness in the skeleton, not buried in a paragraph. 'The one place the test is dangerous' (`the-painkiller-test`)

**Labels only for real sequences.** 'Squeeze one:', 'Step 2:' when the order is the content, never to make a list look like an essay.

Never: 'Conclusion', 'Summary', 'Final thoughts', 'What is X?', 'Why X matters', 'Benefits of X'.

## Closers

**The callback.** Return to the opening's image or claim with one thing changed. Use it when the opening was a scene or a number. 'The prompt on 6 August was one line because everything else was already somewhere.' (`how-i-run-claude-code-with-solo-and-library`)

**The epigram from the argument.** One short line that restates the claim in its hardest form, built from words the piece already used. 'Watch the knobs, not the weights. That's where the story is.' (`opus-didnt-get-worse`)

**The pitch line.** Compress the piece's value into one sentence. Use it for how-tos and setups. 'Nine features while I worked on other things. That's the pitch.' (`my-simplified-ralph-loop-setup-for-claude-code`)

**The admission.** Close on the limit of the argument or what you still get wrong. Use it for reflective pieces. 'I'm still not as good at it as I should be. Nobody is.' (`the-painkiller-test`)

**The step up.** Generalise one level beyond the case, only when the piece earned it. 'The hardest part is never the negotiation. It's admitting you've outgrown the thing you built.' (`why-i-decided-to-sell-my-web-agency`)

**The next action.** For announcements and how-tos only: the install command or the one thing to do now. Never in an opinion piece.

Never: a question to the reader, 'hope this helps', 'thanks for reading', a recap, 'stay tuned', a numbered list of takeaways.
